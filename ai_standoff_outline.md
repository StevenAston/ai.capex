# The AI Standoff: Who pays for progress?

Capital has committed to an AI transformation whose returns may depend on human contributions it has not fully priced—and on cooperation it cannot safely assume.

Compiled from the full imported AI Capex conversation, the Shadow Capital working paper and selected primary research. Steve’s arguments, empirical findings, modelling assumptions and editorial connections are labelled separately. This is a working thesis, not a forecast of a crash.

## 01. Capital has moved first.

The story begins with commitments made before the productive transition is complete.

### 1. The wager concerns the timing of returns, not simply whether AI works. [Mechanism]

Data centres, processors, connections and contracts commit present resources against future applications. The investment thesis requires not only capability but uptake, pricing power and useful operating lives. A capable technology can arrive on a different timetable from the one its financing assumed.

- Separate announcements, contracted commitments, construction and actual cash spent.
- An investment can disappoint even when the underlying tool has real value.

### 2. “AI capex” is an accounting boundary, not a self-explanatory total. [Evidence]

Microsoft’s FY2026 Q4 call reported $41B in capital expenditures and $35.8B in cash paid for property and equipment. Its infrastructure serves AI and non-AI uses. Lease treatment and asset mix matter when comparing companies or years.

- Do not mix fiscal and calendar years, or count all cloud investment as AI-only.
- Use company disclosures to separate cash purchases, leases and other commitments.

Sources: S1

### 3. Strong earnings do not settle project payback. [Mechanism]

Cash is spent before depreciation recognizes the full cost in earnings. Established businesses may finance long experiments. Weak incremental AI returns can reduce valuations without making every incumbent insolvent; a highly leveraged developer and a cash-generative platform have different risks.

- Compare incremental receipts with operations, replacement costs and financing.
- Distinguish a stock-market loss, a funding problem and bankruptcy.

### 4. Steve’s probability is a premise to stress-test. [Perspective]

The original conversation assigns roughly 40–50% to a severe market dislocation. That is Steve’s subjective assessment, not an estimated probability established by this synthesis. The useful exercise is to specify the causal chain and identify observations that strengthen or weaken it.

- Do not turn conviction into a numerical forecast without an explicit model.
- Preserve plausible good outcomes as well as failure paths.

Sources: S0

## 02. A lead is not yet a moat.

The competitive question is whether today’s builders can retain tomorrow’s surplus.

### 5. First-mover advantage must be demonstrated in this market. [Hypothesis]

Being early can build distribution, learning and customer integration. It can also mean paying the highest construction and experimentation costs. “First movers rarely win” is too broad to settle this case; the mechanism of durable advantage needs to be named.

- Examine switching costs, distribution, proprietary data and unit economics.
- Compare entry costs with the value of the installed base rather than relying on a historical slogan.

### 6. International competition can erode returns without eliminating usefulness. [Hypothesis]

Steve’s challenge about China asks whether a temporary capability lead justifies long-lived rents. Months-behind claims need dated, task-matched benchmarks, costs and deployment evidence. Model capability, chips, software ecosystems and industrial implementation are different competitive dimensions.

- A cheaper competitor can expand social benefit while reducing incumbent margins.
- Do not infer complete convergence from one benchmark or complete insulation from export controls.

Sources: S0

### 7. The railroad analogy needs an asset-life adjustment. [Synthesis]

Railway rights-of-way, fibre, buildings, power infrastructure and processors have different useful lives and reuse options. Steve’s concern is that a rapidly changing compute fleet may have a shorter return window than the historical infrastructure analogies suggest.

- The claim concerns economic competitiveness as well as physical functionality.
- Assess each asset class separately rather than treating the whole data centre as a GPU.

### 8. A four-year life and 25% depreciation are not interchangeable. [Model]

A four-year straight-line schedule writes an asset down by 25% of original cost each year. A 25% geometric decline leaves about 31.6% after four years. Neither convention measures actual resale value or replacement necessity. The conversation’s asset-life figures should be scenarios until supported by fleet evidence.

- Distinguish accounting life, physical survival and economic obsolescence.
- The estimated one-third building share is an assumption requiring project-specific validation.

Sources: S0

## 03. Follow the obligation, not only the logo.

Debt, leases, guarantees and intermediaries determine where a disappointment lands.

### 9. A project structure can move the legal claim while leaving economic exposure. [Mechanism]

Joint ventures and special-purpose vehicles can distribute ownership and risk. Guarantees, take-or-pay leases, support agreements or concentrated customers may preserve exposure elsewhere. An off-balance-sheet label alone does not show who ultimately absorbs a loss.

- Map borrower, owner, tenant, guarantor, lender and collateral.
- Separate accounting classification from the full set of economic commitments.

Sources: S14

### 10. Private credit adds a valuation and liquidity question. [Evidence]

NAIC describes private credit as negotiated debt that can have less liquid secondary markets and less frequent pricing. This can suit long-term liabilities when properly managed. It can also make concentration, valuation and exit risk harder to observe.

- Do not equate private credit with fraud or automatic insolvency.
- Identify underwriting, covenants, maturity, collateral and independently assessed value.

Sources: S15

### 11. Insurance and offshore reinsurance deserve a contract-level investigation. [Research]

The conversation raises the possibility of insurer capital financing AI exposure through complex structures. Treasury and NAIC have discussed private credit and offshore reserve transfers. That validates the broader oversight question, not a particular AI-loss estimate or a claim that a loophole has removed responsibility.

- Identify actual insurer holdings and counterparties before claiming hundreds of billions at risk.
- Reinsurance does not mean obligations simply disappear; collateral and supervisory treatment vary.

Sources: S15, S16

### 12. The financing network is a stronger systemic test than capex alone. [Evidence]

S&P’s October 2026 dashboard covers 32 rated North American digital-infrastructure transactions and $105.3B of debt issuance. This is a defined rated universe, not the entire sector or insurer exposure. Its existence illustrates why corporate, project and structured financing need to be traced together.

- Avoid adding different transaction universes or counting the same exposure twice.
- Systemic fragility depends on leverage, concentration and loss absorption.

Sources: S14

## 04. The machine needs a world around it.

“Solow v2.0” is the claim that usable productivity requires changing how work is done.

### 13. A capability demonstration is not a complete production process. [Mechanism]

A model may draft quickly while review, correction or integration absorbs the saving. The meaningful unit is an accepted workflow at a specified quality and risk level. Existing bottlenecks can keep local speedups from changing organizational output.

- Measure rework, error severity, supervision and total elapsed time.
- Separate the transition period from mature operation.

### 14. The productivity J-curve supplies a serious alternative to failure. [Evidence]

General-purpose technologies require complementary organizational investment that can be intangible and poorly measured. That can delay apparent returns. It also raises the question of who performs and pays for the complementary work.

- A historical lag does not guarantee any specific AI project eventually pays back.
- Uncapitalized implementation labour is still a real cost.

Sources: S2

### 15. Task-level productivity findings differ. [Evidence]

The 2023 customer-support study reported roughly 14% higher issues resolved per hour. METR found 19% longer task completion for 16 experienced developers using early-2025 tools on 246 tasks. These are setting-specific findings, not opposing verdicts on the whole economy.

- Specify task, quality standard, experience and model vintage.
- Do not extrapolate one trial into a universal productivity multiplier.

Sources: S8, S9

### 16. Evidence must move with the tools. [Evidence]

METR’s February 2026 update suggests later tools became more useful, but selection and time-measurement problems make the later experiment unreliable for estimating the magnitude. The lesson is to measure actual work repeatedly, not freeze either the optimistic or pessimistic result.

- Self-reported savings and measured productivity are different outcomes.
- Benchmark capability and workplace deployment should inform each other.

Sources: S10, S11

## 05. The computer arrived twice.

Once in enterprise accounts. Again in the homes and evenings of the workforce.

### 17. Households helped make computing familiar. [Evidence]

Historical Census data report computer ownership in 8.2% of U.S. households in 1984 and 15.0% in 1989. Ownership documents diffusion; it does not directly reveal whether a purchase or an hour of use served an employer.

- Identify exposure by year, population and use.
- Do not substitute ownership for occupational motivation.

Sources: S3

### 18. The corporate ledger was not the whole transition. [Research]

Enterprises bought equipment, paid staff and funded training. Households also supplied hardware, internet access, materials and time. The paper asks whether the work-related portion lowered enterprise digitization costs without appearing as enterprise investment.

- Count employer reimbursement and paid training rather than assuming zero.
- The target is a work-attributable share, not all consumer computing expenditure.

Sources: S4

### 19. Adaptation was recurrent rather than one qualification. [Synthesis]

New operating systems, formats, software and connections required maintenance as well as initial learning. Skills could transfer, depreciate or be relearned. That makes the historical burden a recurring-cost question, not just a tuition bill.

- Separate learning, troubleshooting, maintenance and unpaid production.
- Avoid counting the same hour or peripheral twice.

### 20. Private benefit and employer benefit can coexist. [Mechanism]

A worker can gain job access, wages, enjoyment and autonomy while a firm avoids training costs or captures part of the resulting surplus. Defensive learning that preserves employability is still a benefit. Its existence does not establish that the bargain was equitable.

- Compare outcomes with a credible alternative rather than equating any benefit with full compensation.
- Labour-market frictions can change who finances training and receives the return.

Sources: S5

## 06. Name the contribution. Then measure it.

The reconciled paper separates household cost, employer benefit and uncompensated capture.

### 21. The calculation now has one common basis. [Reconciled]

Using the exported arrays for 1980–2010, corporate IT totals $9.852T. With 40% career attribution, ancillary-inclusive household cost is $707.20B. With 1.5 weekly learning hours and a 0.75 wage factor, imputed time is $1.90449T.

- All totals are cumulative nominal USD, not present values or surviving capital.
- The source arrays remain provisional; consistent arithmetic does not authenticate them.

Sources: S4

### 22. Cash and time are complementary categories. [Reconciled]

Combined gross cost is $2.61169T, equivalent in scale to 26.51% of the corporate denominator. Cash and time are not inherently lower and upper bounds on one transfer. The unexplained 1.815 multiplier has been removed.

- Without ancillary expenditure, direct cost is $591.36B and the combined ratio is 25.33%.
- Ancillary inputs need an overlap audit against hardware, books and tuition.

Sources: S4

### 23. Gross household cost is not yet a net subsidy. [Research]

Attribution must distinguish work from personal consumption. A wage-based time value is a convention, not observed cash saved by firms. Employer benefit requires a counterfactual; net capture also requires accounting for reimbursement and worker returns.

- Report learning hours before assigning dollars.
- Keep investment flows, capital stocks, productive services and income distinct.

Sources: S4

### 24. The plausible scale is sensitive to assumptions. [Model]

The baseline implies 180.921B learning hours over 31 years. Selected joint scenarios span 7.88% to 79.50% of the corporate denominator. Those values illustrate sensitivity, not a confidence interval, identified lower bound or probability distribution.

- Authenticate each annual input, interpolation, wage series and exposure definition.
- Vary attribution, hours and time valuation independently, and avoid tuning inputs to recover a headline.

Sources: S4

## 07. Productivity has a distribution.

Who supplied the enabling work, and who received the resulting surplus?

### 25. Labour helped make the technology productive. [Thesis]

The project challenges an account in which capital alone authors productivity and labour is merely a cost. Invention, public knowledge, infrastructure, adaptation and practical judgment jointly produce output. Recognizing an overlooked contribution changes the distributional question without fixing an exact share.

- Distinguish contribution, ownership, market remuneration and fairness.
- Identify specific enabling work rather than attributing all growth to one factor.

### 26. The productivity–pay gap needs consistent measures. [Evidence]

BLS separates relative-price effects from changes in labour share. Its historical 1987–2015 data show software publishers’ labour share falling from 53% to 30%, and wireless carriers’ from 26% to 10%. These selected industries do not establish a household-learning cause.

- Match benefits, workers, sectors, hours and deflators.
- Average compensation, median earnings and labour share answer different questions.

Sources: S6

### 27. Steve’s moral claim is stronger than a neutral accounting claim. [Perspective]

Steve describes workers’ gains as having been appropriated by an elite, and sees a workforce pushed toward a breaking point. That political interpretation belongs in the narrative. Testing it requires defining the return at issue and identifying how gains and costs were distributed.

- Private wage or consumption benefits do not settle whether the bargain was fair.
- Their existence does challenge a literal assertion that every worker received nothing.

Sources: S0

### 28. Productivity can become a personal loss under particular institutions. [Thesis]

Redundancy, intensified work, surveillance or weaker bargaining can make an improvement harmful to its contributor. Under different arrangements, the same capability could deliver shorter hours or higher pay. Even a small uncompensated contribution can matter at national scale, but magnitude alone does not identify extraction.

- Measure security, effort and autonomy as well as wages.
- A social average does not compensate the particular person who bears the loss.

## 08. A worker can know a machine the size of a city.

The earlier discussion of assembly-line expertise belongs inside the economic argument.

### 29. Repeated work can build knowledge of a partly hidden system. [Perspective]

Steve describes people who spent decades across assembly-line roles and became exceptional practical problem solvers. This is an observation to investigate, not a representative survey. Dependencies and failure signatures can become visible through sustained participation.

- Compare official procedure with what experienced workers do when it fails.
- Use interviews and process observation to identify the expertise.

### 30. A narrow station does not imply narrow cognition. [Thesis]

Designing a task to be simple does not measure the abilities of the person doing it. Automation may replicate the routine step while missing exception handling, coordination and recognition that the stated problem is wrong.

- Test complete processes, including abnormal conditions and recovery.
- Avoid assuming either complete interchangeability or universal indispensability.

### 31. Credentials can fail to measure situated competence. [Hypothesis]

Some expertise depends on years inside a particular system. Steve’s “uncredentialable” concept is strongest as a claim about incomplete measurement. The further claim that credentials deliberately preserve elite reproduction needs separate institutional evidence.

- Difficult certification does not imply knowledge can never be taught or assessed.
- Study whether pay, hiring and promotion ignore demonstrated practical competence.

### 32. Offshoring can relocate or dissolve a learning system. [Hypothesis]

Moving production can transfer skills abroad, disrupt design–production feedback or destroy relationships. That is a plausible innovation mechanism. Coincidence with weak domestic productivity does not establish causation, and knowledge transferred abroad is different from knowledge lost altogether.

- Use plant and supplier comparisons with pre-trends and alternative explanations.
- Separate domestic distributional loss from changes in global productive capacity.

## 09. Adoption is not consent to the bargain.

People can want the tool and distrust the terms under which it is introduced.

### 33. The remembered enthusiasm gap motivates a test. [Hypothesis]

Steve recalls the internet era as widely optimistic, with households eager to learn even as employers worried about cost. He contrasts that with current threats of displacement and public hostility. This is a research lead requiring matched historical evidence, not an established cross-era statistic.

- Compare expectations of autonomy, security, benefits and learning burden.
- Investor excitement and selected CEO remarks are not representative worker attitudes.

Sources: S0

### 34. Rapid AI use is counterevidence to a blanket refusal story. [Evidence]

Adoption research documents fast diffusion. A person can use AI frequently while opposing surveillance or unpaid workplace adaptation. Criticism cannot be read directly as withholding, and a login cannot be read as productive integration.

- Separate personal use, employer-authorized use, intensity and disclosure.
- Match survey date, population and definition before comparing AI with PCs or the internet.

Sources: S7, S11

### 35. Displacement framing can change the payoff to cooperation. [Hypothesis]

Learning may look attractive if it improves employability. Teaching a substitute while retaining the costs and losing the benefits can look threatening. Some workers might instead accelerate adoption defensively, so the direction and scale of the effect must be measured.

- Measure credible company policy separately from generalized fear.
- Distinguish private use of AI from employer-directed knowledge extraction.

### 36. Public legitimacy is a separate constraint. [Evidence]

The conversation links labour distrust with resistance to data-centre construction. Power, water, local costs and governance provide other explanations. S&P identifies permitting and public pushback as constraints; it does not establish the conversation’s blanket claim that projects over 50 MW cannot be built.

- Treat that size threshold as an unverified generalization.
- Test local burdens and public benefits rather than inferring one motive for all opposition.

Sources: S14, S12

## 10. A standoff needs no organized strike.

Millions of individually understandable choices can create an invisible coordination problem.

### 37. Aligned interests can produce similar behaviour without coordination. [Hypothesis]

The central analogy is incentive alignment: workers can independently ask why they should train a replacement. Reduced unpaid adaptation or voluntary knowledge sharing could slow deployment without a formal labour action. The existence and prevalence of this behaviour are hypotheses.

- This concerns lawful choices about effort, disclosure and participation, not sabotage.
- A failed rollout alone is not evidence of withholding.

Sources: S0

### 38. The expected payoff can be made explicit. [Model]

A simplified worker payoff is personal benefit plus expected share of gains, minus learning cost and expected losses of security or autonomy. Cooperation becomes more attractive when this balance improves relative to alternatives. Pride, ethics and reciprocity also matter.

- The model organizes the question; it is not an estimated behavioural law.
- Benefits can include time, prospects, wages and control.

### 39. Credibility may be the missing complement. [Hypothesis]

A promise of shared benefit may be discounted if management can reverse it after extracting the workflow. Specific, credible commitments could matter more than exhortations to be enthusiastic. The theory predicts a role for trust and enforceability, not simply optimism.

- Compare generic reassurance with concrete gain-sharing and transition terms.
- Measure belief in the arrangement, not just policy existence.

### 40. Paying for cooperation may be productive investment. [Proposal]

Paid learning, worker involvement, transparent evaluation and enforceable sharing arrangements are candidate interventions. They have costs and can fail. Their value should be judged by net output, quality and worker outcomes rather than assumed in advance.

- Identify which contribution is compensated and which benefit is enforceable.
- Compare against an ethically acceptable rollout with paid duties.

## 11. The missing return can travel.

An adoption delay can become a financing problem through identifiable channels.

### 41. Slower uptake can compress the return window. [Scenario]

Cash may arrive later while hardware ages and commitments continue. Strong demand or flexible spending could offset this, but the pathway from delayed organizational change to weak utilization is economically coherent.

- Track realized prices, utilization, retention, margins and replacement spending.
- A backlog is not cash collected or proof of customer profitability.

### 42. Losses can propagate without every hyperscaler failing. [Scenario]

A spending contraction can affect suppliers, construction, leases, credit and collateral. The scale depends on leverage, funding maturity and who absorbs losses. Institutions holding common exposures may transmit a shock even when the largest platforms survive.

- Separate equity repricing from defaults and a systemic credit squeeze.
- Trace guarantees and funding conditions instead of treating all companies alike.

### 43. The physical asset and its financial claim can have different fates. [Mechanism]

A data-centre owner may lose capital while another operator reuses power infrastructure, fibre or buildings. Specialized equipment and unsuitable sites can be harder to repurpose. Useful capacity can survive a write-down; sunk cost alone does not guarantee social value.

- Assess conversion cost and site-specific alternative demand.
- Separate preserving capacity from guaranteeing original investor returns.

### 44. A financial bust can interact with material supply shocks. [Scenario]

The conversation considers credit contraction alongside constrained energy, fertilizer or climate-sensitive supply. Financial support cannot immediately create those physical inputs. This is a compound scenario, not a verified claim that an unprecedented El Niño or a particular geopolitical outcome is inevitable.

- Model inflation, recession and policy response as distinct interacting paths.
- Do not inherit the earlier conversation’s unsourced 1,000- or 10,000-year rarity claims.

Sources: S0

## 12. Protection is a set of competing objectives.

The original question about wealth becomes clearer when the crisis scenarios are separated.

### 45. Liquidity, purchasing power and nominal safety are different goals. [Framework]

An asset may retain nominal value yet lose purchasing power. A long-lived real asset may be valuable but hard to sell during a crisis. The earlier question is best treated as a stress test of obligations and exposures before selecting a hedge.

- Define the timing and currency of essential spending.
- Do not confuse the survival of a financial intermediary with protection against market losses.

### 46. Employment income can be correlated with the portfolio. [Framework]

Exposure is not limited to a directly held technology stock. Income, pension holdings, market-weighted funds, private credit and local property may share a dependency on the investment cycle. Apparent diversification needs to be tested at the level of economic risk.

- Map holdings and income sources before inferring concentration.
- Index membership count does not measure independence of risks.

### 47. A commodity thesis can contain both long-run demand and near-term drawdown. [Perspective]

Steve considers copper and silver as material-demand ideas. A capex contraction could still weaken prices, and mine equities introduce leverage, costs and operating risks beyond the commodity itself. The earlier price-timing thesis is not adopted here as a current forecast.

- Separate physical demand, supply response, financing and valuation.
- No present price target or trade recommendation follows from this narrative.

Sources: S0

### 48. No single hedge dominates every version of the scenario. [Framework]

Deflationary credit contraction, inflationary supply stress and currency weakness reward different properties. The narrative preserves that uncertainty rather than repeating the earlier illustrative portfolio allocation as personalized advice.

- Stress-test duration, liquidity, currency, leverage and correlated income loss.
- An unverified crash probability is insufficient to optimize a portfolio.

## 13. The hidden ledger extends beyond labour.

The energy work supplies a parallel: a component’s price is not the cost of complete service.

### 49. Physical complements constrain what money can buy. [Evidence]

The IEA’s 2026 update estimates all data centres used 485 TWh in 2025 and projects 950 TWh in 2030. These are not AI-only totals. Grid connections, supply chains and social acceptance can constrain deployment.

- Distinguish annual energy, peak power, location and timing.
- Efficient queries can coexist with rising total demand.

Sources: S12

### 50. Hourly adequacy illustrates the same boundary discipline. [Synthesis]

The prior 1 GW, 8,760-hour energy model asks for continuous service rather than annual generation alone. Outages, storage, reserves, transmission and weather matter. Its numerical conclusions are contextual here, not newly audited.

- One shortfall-free weather year is not a probabilistic reliability guarantee.
- Likewise, low cost per token is not low cost per reliable completed workflow.

### 51. Private accounts can exclude enabling costs borne elsewhere. [Hypothesis]

Household adaptation, grid upgrades or public guarantees can support an investment. Each channel needs separate evidence. Not every grid upgrade is a subsidy; public contributions can earn public benefits. The question is the allocation of incremental cost and risk.

- Trace tariffs, contracts, guarantees, reimbursements and public returns.
- Avoid collapsing different cost-shifting mechanisms into one unsupported dollar total.

Sources: S12

### 52. Opportunity cost matters even if the buildout leaves useful assets. [Thesis]

Engineering capacity, finance and materials used in one sector are unavailable elsewhere at that moment. Steve’s comparison with power stations and manufacturing is an allocation question. It requires comparing feasible alternatives, not assuming every AI dollar would otherwise fund a reactor.

- Evaluate displaced projects, supply constraints and alternative social returns.
- The common goal is useful capacity and human flourishing, not investment volume alone.

## 14. The thesis must survive its strongest alternatives.

A persuasive narrative needs clear conditions under which it would be wrong.

### 53. Technical bottlenecks may explain most deployment friction. [Counterargument]

Reliability, data, regulation, procurement and management can slow implementation regardless of worker incentives. The standoff hypothesis earns its place only if it explains additional variation after these factors are considered.

- Do not interpret every delay as resistance.
- Compare similar tasks and organizations with differing credible benefit-sharing terms.

### 54. More autonomous tools could reduce the cooperation requirement. [Counterargument]

Better models, standardized environments and off-the-shelf workflows may reduce dependence on local employee knowledge. Conversely, some deployments may remain deeply contextual. The claim should predict where its own mechanism is weak.

- Expect weaker effects in standardized tasks with large private benefits.
- Reassess as capabilities and costs change rather than treating current dependence as permanent.

### 55. Workers can receive meaningful benefits without owning the system. [Evidence]

Tools may reduce drudgery, create opportunities or improve consumer purchasing power. Early labour-market studies may find modest earnings or hours effects. These possibilities must be included without treating an average benefit as compensation for every loss.

- Estimate heterogeneous outcomes and long-run adjustment.
- Early Danish null results constrain immediate broad displacement claims, not all future effects.

Sources: S13

### 56. A useful, competitive AI market is also a plausible outcome. [Counterargument]

Technical improvements, lower costs, new demand and better institutions could support substantial productivity without a systemic bust. Investor returns might still vary widely. The framework separates successful deployment, financial sustainability and distribution instead of treating them as one axis.

- Track both confirming and disconfirming indicators.
- Do not assign scenario probabilities merely to make the narrative feel quantitative.

## 15. Make the standoff observable.

The argument becomes stronger when it tells us what to measure.

### 57. Measure cooperation separately from attitudes and use. [Test]

Record paid and unpaid learning, voluntary documentation, exception reporting, private-use disclosure and quality-adjusted output. A survey complaint is not a refusal; a login is not productive implementation.

- Measure security, trust, task suitability and expected benefits.
- Protect confidentiality and prevent punitive use of honest reports.

### 58. Randomize the terms of rollout, not just tool access. [Test]

With consent and an acceptable paid baseline, compare ordinary rollout with credible benefit sharing or transition commitments. A factorial design can separate training support from the distributional promise. Randomize teams when spillovers are likely.

- Pre-register output quality, worker outcomes, implementation costs and follow-up.
- Use pilot variance to determine statistical power rather than inventing a sample size.

### 59. Rebuild the historical estimate from source observations. [Test]

The reconciled paper now makes arithmetic reproducible. The next stage is verifying each annual source value, identifying interpolation, removing overlap and measuring private returns. A cleaner total is the start of validation rather than its completion.

- Retain table, release vintage, units, coverage and transformation for every observation.
- Do not describe scenario ranges as confidence intervals or novelty as proven without a literature review.

Sources: S4

### 60. Name the result that would weaken the theory. [Test]

The mechanism weakens if credible gain sharing does not affect behaviour or net outcomes, if technical constraints explain the gaps, or if adoption succeeds with little discretionary knowledge. It gains support if terms affect cooperation beyond training and tool quality, especially in high-context, threatened tasks.

- Study mediation through trust and voluntary contribution.
- Seek negative cases and heterogeneous effects rather than only matching anecdotes.

## 16. The terms of progress are part of its machinery.

The concluding proposition is institutional as well as technological.

### 61. Compensate the inputs the transition actually requires. [Proposal]

If learning, evaluation and documentation are necessary work, include them in implementation budgets. Calling them enthusiasm does not make them costless. Compensation should match identifiable contributions without assuming every personal computing activity belongs on an employer’s ledger.

- Specify paid time, reimbursement and responsibility.
- Evaluate quality-adjusted gains after all relevant costs.

### 62. Give people a credible stake in the gains. [Proposal]

Pay, reduced hours, transition security and worker influence are possible forms of return. Their effects and trade-offs should be tested. A promise that cannot survive implementation may have little value to the people being asked to cooperate.

- Prefer specific, enforceable arrangements over generic assurances.
- Measure worker benefit as well as employer savings.

### 63. Preserve practical expertise while introducing new tools. [Proposal]

Documenting and sharing knowledge need not mean discarding the people who generated it. Deployment can combine new capability with learning, apprenticeship and resilient operations. Whether that arrangement is more productive than replacement is an empirical question.

- Evaluate error recovery, continuity and long-run learning.
- Do not mistake removal of a payroll line for removal of a productive dependency.

### 64. The central wager may be on an underpriced human contribution. [Thesis]

The narrative ends where it began: capital expects future cash from technical capacity. If those returns depend on adaptation and cooperation, their distribution can affect whether the returns arrive. That is a conditional, testable theory of the AI standoff—not a demand that useful technological progress stop.

- Distinguish what is known, assumed, hypothesized and morally claimed.
- Judge progress by durable productive capacity and human benefit.
